Curled Metal: From Customer Value to Price

What should CMI charge for its 11.5-inch cushion pad at launch, and how will it get contractors to try it?

Rajiv Sanwal is about to quote Kendrick a price, and your team is advising him. Use the numbers and details in the case. Your recommendation should hold up for the customer, for CMI, and for whoever sits in between.

Working plan: Task 1: 25 minutes • Task 2: 20 minutes • Task 3: 15 minutes • Task 4: 10 minutes • Class report-outs and discussion: 20 minutes.

Download the report-out workbook

Team output: One calculation table and one recommendation slide (or page). Pick a spokesperson to give a three-minute summary and take questions. Your instructor will call on a few teams, and may ask some of them to compare how they handled a particular step.

Task 1 — Estimate Customer Economic Value

Pick Kendrick or Corey. Work out the most that customer could spend on CMI pads for the job and still come out even compared with conventional pads. Then divide that by the number of CMI pads to get a per-pad figure. Keep in mind that this is a ceiling based on the math. It isn’t what the buyer believes the pads are worth, and it isn’t what they’ll actually pay.

  1. For both conventional and CMI pads, figure out feet driven, driving hours, and pad-change hours. Use the change counts from the test tables, and don’t round until the end.
  2. Put a dollar value on the time saved using Table A’s average cost per real hour for equipment and labor. Leave out the $300/hour overhead for now, but be ready to say whether any of it would really go away.
  3. Add what the customer no longer spends on conventional pads. Divide the total by the number of CMI pads used.
  4. Saving hours doesn’t automatically save money. What would have to happen for it to? And why shouldn’t crane moves and other delays shrink just because driving went faster?

Task 2 — Recommend a Price CMI Can Deliver Profitably

Choose a launch price per pad, as the end customer would pay it. Explain where it sits relative to the value you calculated, what buyers are likely to believe, and how much of the savings the customer gets to keep.

  • Customer side: How much does the customer save on the job at your price? Which benefits will buyers accept right away, and which will they want to see proven?
  • CMI side: Use Exhibit 6. Say whether you’re assuming CMI uses its existing equipment or invests in permanent tooling. Keep variable cost and total manufacturing cost separate. Does your price reach the 40–50% margin the case mentions?
  • Channel: Is CMI selling direct or through a distributor? If it’s a distributor, assume a gross margin and work back to CMI’s price. Note any rep commissions or selling costs you left out.
  • Decision: Your price probably won’t match the value ceiling or a cost-plus number. Explain why. What would you need to learn to change it?
Watch which price you’re talking about. The distributor’s margin is a share of its selling price, not a markup on what it paid. CMI’s margin is figured on CMI’s selling price. Label every price and assumption so nobody has to guess.

Task 3 — Earn Buyer and Channel Support

Pick three people or groups in the case who influence whether CMI pads get bought. At least one should be likely to push back. For each one:

  • What part do they play: buying, specifying, recommending, or distributing?
  • What do they gain or lose, in money or reputation, if CMI pads catch on?
  • What would it take to win them over: proof, a demo, or an incentive?

Conclude: What should CMI do first to educate buyers? Say who it should approach, what it should show them, and how it will know the effort worked. Keep in mind that encouraging early test results aren’t the same as an independent endorsement.

Task 4 — Test High- and Low-Price Strategies

Make the best case you can for each approach, then choose one. First, say what “high” and “low” mean compared with your price and the customer value you calculated.

  • Go high if… What would have to be true about customers, the channel, competitors, and CMI itself for this to work?
  • Go low if… When would it make sense to give up margin per pad in exchange for faster adoption or more volume?

Test both arguments against three questions. Will buyers and distributors go along with it? Could competitors copy the pad or work around it, given that the patent may not hold up? Does CMI have the sales effort, capacity, and cash to carry it out? Also think about why a low price alone might not get contractors to switch or keep competitors out.

Conclude: What’s the shakiest assumption behind the strategy you chose? How could CMI check it, and what result would make you change course?

Report-Out — Make the Launch Decision

Your calculation table is your backup. Your slide should cover:

  1. Price per pad to the end customer, how CMI sells it, and CMI’s own selling price
  2. Customer value and net savings for the job you picked
  3. CMI’s manufacturing margin, given your equipment and channel assumptions
  4. The person or group you’d focus on first, and how you’d win them over
  5. Your pricing strategy, the assumption it depends on most, and what would change your mind

What your instructor is looking for: units and price levels that line up, a clear line between what the case says and what you assumed, an honest look at who wins and who loses, and a decision that follows from your numbers.
