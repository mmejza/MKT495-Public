MKT 495 • Class 9 Breakout Activity

Curled Metal: From Customer Value to Price

Recommend a launch price for CMI’s 11.5-inch cushion pad and defend how the company will earn adoption.

Your team advises Rajiv Sanwal before he quotes a price to Kendrick. Use case evidence to connect customer economics, CMI’s costs, channel incentives, and competitive conditions.

Working plan: Task 1: 25 minutes • Task 2: 20 minutes • Task 3: 15 minutes • Task 4: 10 minutes • Class report-outs and discussion: 20 minutes.

Download the report-out workbook

Team output: One shared calculation table and one recommendation slide or page. Choose a spokesperson; be ready to give a three-minute report and answer questions. Your instructor will select teams to present or compare specific parts of their analysis.

Task 1 — Estimate Customer Economic Value

Choose either Kendrick or Corey. Estimate the maximum total price for the CMI pads used on that job at which the customer would break even relative to conventional pads. Then express that amount per CMI pad. This economic-value estimate is a benchmark; it does not establish what a buyer actually believes or will pay.

  1. Calculate feet actually driven, driving hours, and pad-change hours for conventional and CMI pads. Use the test tables’ reported change counts and convert minutes to hours without rounding intermediate results.
  2. Value time saved using Table A’s average cost per real hour for equipment and labor. For the base calculation, exclude the $300/hour overhead allocation; discuss whether any of it would actually be avoided.
  3. Add the conventional-pad purchases avoided. Divide the resulting job-level value by the number of CMI pads consumed.
  4. State what must be true for saved time to become cash savings or valuable additional capacity. Explain why unrelated crane-moving time and delays should not automatically fall with driving time.

Task 2 — Recommend a Price CMI Can Deliver Profitably

Recommend an end-customer purchase price per pad for launch. Explain how it relates to economic value, perceived value, adoption risk, and the savings retained by the customer.

  • Customer economics: Calculate the customer’s net job savings at your price. Which benefits will buyers believe immediately, and which require proof?
  • CMI economics: Use Exhibit 6. State whether you assume existing equipment or permanent tooling. Distinguish variable manufacturing cost from total manufacturing cost, and assess the case’s 40–50% margin expectation after manufacturing costs.
  • Route to market: State whether the initial sale is direct or through a distributor. For a distributor sale, specify an assumed distributor gross margin and calculate CMI’s selling price to the distributor. Identify any representative commissions or other selling costs excluded from your calculation.
  • Decision: Explain why your recommended price need not equal either the economic-value benchmark or a cost-based price. Identify one finding that would cause you to change it.
Keep the price levels distinct. Distributor gross margin is a percentage of the distributor’s selling price, not a markup on its purchase cost. CMI’s manufacturing margin uses CMI’s own selling price. Label each price, cost basis, and assumption.

Task 3 — Earn Buyer and Channel Support

Choose three important buying influences from the case, including at least one potential source of resistance. For each, identify:

  • Their role in buying, specifying, recommending, or distributing pads.
  • How CMI pads help or threaten their economic interests or professional reputation.
  • The evidence, demonstration, or incentive most likely to earn their support.

Conclude: Propose CMI’s first buyer-education action: whom to approach, what to demonstrate, and what result would count as success. Distinguish promising preliminary test feedback from a completed independent endorsement.

Task 4 — Test High- and Low-Price Strategies

Develop a case-supported argument for each strategy, then select the one that best supports your recommendation. Define what “high” and “low” mean relative to your proposed price and customer value.

  • CMI should set a high price if… Explain the customer, channel, competitive, and internal conditions that would make this strategy work.
  • CMI should set a low price if… Explain the conditions that would justify accepting less value per pad to pursue adoption or volume.

Test both arguments against external conditions (buyer acceptance and channel incentives), industry dynamics (entry, imitation, substitute technologies, and uncertain patent protection), and CMI’s capabilities (selling effort, capacity, and financing). Explain why low price alone may not overcome adoption barriers or deter entry.

Conclude: Identify the most important uncertain assumption behind your chosen strategy, a way to test it, and a result that would trigger a different pricing decision.

Report-Out — Make the Launch Decision

Use your calculation table as supporting evidence. On one recommendation slide or page, summarize:

  1. Your end-customer price per pad, route to market, and CMI selling price.
  2. Customer economic value and net savings for your selected job.
  3. CMI manufacturing margin under your stated equipment and channel assumptions.
  4. Your priority buying influence and first action to earn adoption.
  5. Your pricing strategy, its most important assumption, and the evidence that would change your decision.

A strong recommendation uses consistent units and price levels, separates case facts from assumptions, accounts for conflicting incentives, and makes a decision that follows from the evidence.
