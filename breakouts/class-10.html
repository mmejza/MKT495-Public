Springfield Nor’easters: Set the First-Season Prices

What should the team charge for tickets, and will ticket sales and concessions cover its costs?

Your team advises Larry Buckingham as he sets prices for the Nor’easters’ first season. Start with the research, build a ticket-revenue estimate, and then test whether the plan breaks even once concessions are included. Use the case exhibits and label any assumptions you make.

Team output: One shared calculation table and one recommendation slide (or page). Pick a spokesperson to give a three-minute summary and take questions. Be ready to explain the steps behind your numbers. Record a brief response to each judgment challenge; use those responses to shape your final recommendation.

Task 1 — Decide What the Research Tells You

Research helps managers make decisions, but it does not make the decision for them. Evaluate each study and explain what it means for a new minor league baseball team in Springfield.

  1. League Sports Association survey (Exhibit 3): What conclusions can you draw about professional sports spectators? Which conclusions are likely to apply to professional baseball spectators, and where would you be cautious?
  2. Buckingham’s 2008 web survey (Exhibit 5): Identify the three to five most important findings for the Nor’easters. For each, explain the implication for ticket pricing, package design, or attendance.

Judgment challenge: Which finding would you trust least when committing real money? Identify a plausible bias, explain whether it would overstate or understate demand, and propose one practical way to check it before opening day. Distinguish what respondents say they will do from what you can confidently expect them to buy.

Task 2 — Establish the Break-Even Target

How much must ticket revenue and concession contribution generate in the first season to cover the team’s costs?

  • List and total the first-season fixed costs the Nor’easters must cover. Show each item and its case source.
  • List and total the non-ticket revenues that can help cover those costs. Keep concessions separate for Task 4.
  • Subtract those other revenues from fixed costs to find the remaining amount needed from ticket sales and concessions.

Conclude: Report the remaining break-even target in dollars. Explain why gross concession sales cannot be treated as profit available to cover fixed costs.

Judgment challenge: Should breaking even in year one be the primary objective? Defend a balance among first-season profit, building a fan base, and keeping baseball affordable. Explain the financial constraint your recommendation must respect.

Task 3 — Choose Ticket Prices and Forecast Revenue

Fans differ in what they will pay and how many games they want to attend. Use Exhibit 5 to find the ticket-revenue-maximizing price for each option: single game, 5 games, half season (20 games), and full season (38 games).

  1. Compare the candidate prices. For each ticket option, calculate the share willing to pay each price or more. Use that cumulative willingness to pay to compare expected ticket revenue across prices. State your selected price per seat per game and the total package price.
  2. Estimate first-season ticket revenue. Use your selected prices, the share interested in each ticket option, and the case’s market-size information to estimate buyers, packages sold, paid seat-games, and ticket revenue. State whether your market base is individuals or households and explain your choice using the case. Avoid counting the same buyer in more than one ticket option.
Keep the units straight. One 20-game package sells 20 seat-games. Multiply packages sold by games per package and price per seat per game to get ticket revenue. A fan willing to pay a higher price is also willing to pay a lower one; use cumulative demand rather than just the percentage naming one price.

Build a calculation table with one row for each ticket option and a total row. Include:

CalculationShow your inputs and result
Potential buyersMarket base × share interested in this ticket option
Buyers at your pricePotential buyers × cumulative share willing to pay your price
Paid seat-gamesPackages sold × games per package; state your packages-per-buyer assumption
Ticket revenuePaid seat-games × price per seat per game

Conclude: Report all four prices and total ticket revenue. Compare projected seat-games with season capacity and explain what that comparison can and cannot tell you about sellouts at individual games. Keep intermediate calculations unrounded.

Judgment challenge: Would you launch at the calculated ticket-revenue-maximizing prices? Defend one reason to depart from them, or explain why you would keep them. Consider how customers will perceive the package discounts and whether packages could draw buyers away from more profitable options. If you recommend different prices, show how they change your forecast.

Task 4 — Add Concessions and Test Profitability

Use the ticket forecast from Task 3 and the case’s attendance and concession information to determine whether your pricing plan makes a first-season profit.

  1. Apply the expected attendance rate for each ticket option to its paid seat-games. Add the results to estimate actual visits to the ballpark.
  2. Use the survey’s spending distribution to estimate average concession spending per attendee. Show the representative amount you use for each spending range.
  3. Calculate gross concession sales, then apply the case’s concession contribution margin to find the amount available to cover fixed costs.
  4. Add ticket revenue and concession contribution, then subtract the remaining break-even target from Task 2. Report the projected profit or loss.

Conclude: Does the pricing plan break even? Test one less favorable attendance or demand assumption and explain whether your recommendation still holds.

Judgment challenge: Which is more valuable: a paid empty seat or an occupied seat sold at a discount? Explain the conditions that determine your answer, including concessions, the game-day atmosphere, and effects on customers who paid full price. Use your calculations where possible, and identify effects your model does not capture.

Report-Out — Recommend the First-Season Plan

Decision scenario: Suppose ownership wants higher ticket prices, while the community-relations manager wants affordable family attendance. Recommend a launch policy both must live with. Identify whose interests you prioritize and the tradeoff you accept.

Use your calculation table as supporting evidence. On one recommendation slide or page, summarize:

  1. The research finding that most influenced your decision and its main limitation.
  2. Your recommended prices for all four ticket options, labeled per game and per package.
  3. Your projected ticket revenue, actual attendance, concession contribution, and profit or loss.
  4. Your response to the decision scenario: whose interests you prioritize and the tradeoff you accept.
  5. The strongest argument against your recommendation and the evidence that would change your decision. Draw on your most important assumption and less favorable scenario.

A strong recommendation connects the research to a decision, shows how the numbers fit together, and defends a tradeoff while taking the strongest opposing argument seriously.
