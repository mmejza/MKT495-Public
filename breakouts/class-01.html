Needs, AI & Market Segmentation

Three connected exercises in customer and market analysis

These exercises explore the distinction between needs and wants, the strengths and limitations of AI-generated analysis, and the managerial purpose of market segmentation. Complete the exercises in order and record your group’s work in the report-out workbook.

Exercise 1 — Needs, Wants & AI

Key Concepts

NeedA state of felt deprivation that occurs within a person.
WantA desire for something that will reduce or remove the state of felt deprivation.
DemandA want for a specific product category combined with the ability to purchase it.
MarketAll buyers with a common need who demand a product category.

Products

  • Wired headphones
  • Wired earbuds
  • Omega dress watches
  • Archie & Oscar Damien pet crate

Part 1 — Human Analysis

Do not use internet search or AI tools during Part 1.
  1. For each product, describe three different needs it could satisfy. Be honest, open-minded, and creative.
  2. Record the group’s descriptions on the Part 1 – Human Analysis worksheet.

Part 2 — AI Comparison

  1. Choose one AI application. Use the prompt supplied in the workbook once for each product.
  2. Paste each response into the Part 2 – AI Critique worksheet.
  3. Without further AI assistance, critically evaluate the responses:
    • What patterns appear across the responses?
    • Which claims are well supported, questionable, vague, or inaccurate?
    • What relevant marketing ideas, concepts, or perspectives are ignored, omitted, or underrepresented?

Exercise 2 — Segmentation

Segmentation is the process of discovering homogeneous groups within a defined population that share characteristics relevant to buying behavior. Useful segmentation should make a specific business decision less difficult.

Task 1 — Evaluate Two Streaming-Market Schemes

Assume a subscription video service is reconsidering its programming, pricing, and promotion. Compare these two possible segmentation schemes.

Scheme A: Who subscribers are

SegmentDefining Characteristics
Campus streamersAge 18–24, enrolled in college, mobile-first viewing.
Young professionalsAge 25–34, employed full time, above-average household income.
Family householdsAge 30–49, children at home, multiple household viewers.
Mature viewersAge 50+, no children at home, primarily television-based viewing.

Scheme B: Why and how subscribers use streaming

SegmentNeeds & Behaviors
Event seekersSubscribe for live sports, news, premieres, and shared cultural moments.
Escape bingersWant immersive entertainment and frequently watch several episodes at once.
Family curatorsNeed safe, varied programming and profiles for multiple household members.
Background companionsUse familiar content while working, cooking, relaxing, or falling asleep.
Value rotatorsSwitch services frequently to control cost and follow specific programs.
  1. What business decisions or problems might have led to each scheme?
  2. Does each scheme provide sufficient information for implementing a marketing strategy? Why or why not?
  3. What does this comparison teach you about useful segmentation?

Task 2 — Same Demographics, Different Automobile Needs

Both buyers are age 35, partnered with one child, college graduates, and live in households earning $100,000 annually. Identify two or three automobile needs and corresponding models for each:

  • Buyer A: commutes 45 miles daily, lives in a city condominium with limited parking, rarely carries passengers, and prioritizes low operating cost and environmental impact.
  • Buyer B: works remotely from a rural home, regularly transports family and outdoor equipment, drives on unpaved roads in winter, and occasionally tows a small trailer.

Which descriptors actually drive the differences in automobile needs? Classify those descriptors as needs, behavior, demographics, or lifestyle.
